title: "Master's thesis: predicting short-term economic growth with deep learning"
lead: "Can a recurrent neural network (RNN) that combines monthly and quarterly data directly 'nowcast' the economy better than the Dynamic Factor Model (DFM), the standard nowcasting model?"
description: Three custom recurrent neural network architectures for nowcasting GDP growth in real time, tested against a Dynamic Factor Model (DFM) and ARMA.
image: /assets/img/projects/scriptie_rmse_vergelijking_en.png
abstract: "Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is published quarterly and with a delay. Monthly figures such as unemployment and inflation arrive much sooner. To improve the predictive power of neural networks, I built a new mixed-frequency model that integrates monthly figures to predict GDP growth of the current quarter before it ends. From 2000 to 2019 the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of my best deep learning model is about 10% lower than that of a DFM: the type of model central banks use, which I applied in a simple form to the same data."

{% include slide.html kicker="Problem" title="What is nowcasting?" text="Nowcasting is an estimate of GDP growth right now, so you do not have to rely on outdated figures. In the US, the first GDP estimate only comes out about a month after the quarter ends. Policymakers, central banks and investors need to decide sooner, especially when the economy changes fast. A nowcast uses the information that is already available. In my model that means monthly figures; central-bank nowcasts often also use, for example, financial market data." src="/assets/img/projects/scriptie_probleem_en.svg" alt="Timeline: monthly figures arrive every month, but first-quarter GDP is only known weeks after the quarter ends" full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Background and models" title="Nowcasting with deep learning" text="Almost every major central bank has a nowcasting model, often a DFM. Machine learning is mostly still being researched and sometimes used alongside the DFM, for example at the International Monetary Fund (IMF), and there are only a handful of deep learning studies for GDP. Those studies usually average the monthly figures into quarters first, which loses information. I therefore designed RNNs that read each monthly figure separately as soon as it is out, and compared them with an RNN on quarterly averages, the DFM and an Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA) model, which uses only past GDP." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_networks_en.svg" alt="The same monthly and quarterly figures over three quarters, fed to two RNN set-ups. The quarterly RNN averages the three months and combines them with the quarterly figures into one input per quarter: it makes a nowcast every quarter, but not while the current quarter is still incomplete. My mixed-frequency RNNs read every month as its own input step, followed by the quarterly figures: they make a nowcast every month, even before the quarter ends." full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Data" title="Many indicators, few observations" text="The dataset contains 238 US indicators: 121 monthly ones from FRED-MD and 117 quarterly ones from FRED-QD. GDP, however, is measured only once per quarter: 260 quarters from 1960 to 2024, of which 160 come before the first test year. With almost as many candidate features as observations, a model can learn the noise in the training data instead of real patterns (overfitting), and then predicts new quarters poorly. I therefore used Least Angle Regression (LARS) to keep the 10 to 20 most informative indicators, repeated on the training data each time the model is retrained. On the first training period (1960–1999), it mainly picked industrial production, capacity utilisation, employment and consumption (right-hand figure). The European Central Bank (ECB) nowcasting toolbox also recommends LARS ([Linzenich & Meunier, 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp3004~3ce9d0d8ca.en.pdf)). The data exploration supports the mixed-frequency idea: as soon as monthly figures from the current quarter come in, they correlate more strongly with this quarter's GDP growth (left-hand figure: 0.35 with one month, against 0.19 with only the previous quarter's months)." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_mpl_corr_monthly.png" alt="Three rows of dots, one dot per monthly indicator, showing its absolute correlation with this quarter's GDP growth. Industrial production and capacity utilisation in manufacturing lead, followed by employment in goods-producing industries, manufacturing output and real personal consumption; three of the 14 are quarterly indicators." full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Result question 3" title="Crises and calm periods: no single winner" text="**Research question 3:** do the models differ in accuracy between recessions and expansions? {% include slide.html kicker="Discussion" title="Caveats and next steps" points="**Smaller effect than expected:** I expected it to help a lot to already have monthly figures for the quarter you are predicting, but the effect was smaller. The network that reads them has just a 4% lower RMSE than the same kind of network on quarterly figures only. Much of the predictive power is already in GDP growth of the past quarters: ARMA, which uses only GDP, was even better than the DFM outside crises. | **Simple comparison model:** my DFM is a simple version on the same data, so this says nothing about the far more extensive nowcasts of central banks. | **Little data:** GDP comes out only four times a year and the series start around 1960, while deep learning needs a lot of data. Possible solutions: training on data from several countries, or adding synthetic data, for example simulated with Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models. | **Next step:** a model that makes a nowcast at any moment, using everything known at that time, such as daily financial market data. It can be tested against the quarterly figure, but that requires real-time data vintages: the data as it was known at each moment, before later revisions." statement=true %}
