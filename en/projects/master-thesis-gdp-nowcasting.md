lead: "Can a recurrent neural network (RNN) that combines monthly and quarterly data directly 'nowcast' the economy better than the Dynamic Factor Model (DFM), the standard nowcasting model?"

abstract: "Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is published quarterly and with a delay. Monthly figures such as unemployment and inflation arrive much sooner. To improve the predictive power of neural networks, I built a new mixed-frequency model that integrates monthly figures to predict GDP growth of the current quarter before it ends. From 2000 to 2019 the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of my best deep learning model is about 10% lower than that of a DFM: the type of model central banks use, which I applied in a simple form to the same data."

{% include slide.html kicker="Problem" title="What is nowcasting?" text="Nowcasting is an estimate of GDP growth right now, so you do not have to rely on outdated figures. In the US, the first GDP estimate only comes out about a month after the quarter ends. Policymakers, central banks and investors need to decide sooner, especially when the economy changes fast. A nowcast uses the information that is already available. In my model that means monthly figures; central-bank nowcasts often also use, for example, financial market data." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_timeline_en_v2.svg" alt="Timeline: monthly figures arrive every month, but first-quarter GDP is only known weeks after the quarter ends" full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Background and models" title="Nowcasting with deep learning" text="Almost every major central bank has a nowcasting model, often a DFM. Machine learning is mostly still being researched and sometimes used alongside the DFM, for example at the International Monetary Fund (IMF), and there are only a handful of deep learning studies for GDP. Those studies usually average the monthly figures into quarters first, which loses information. I therefore designed monthly + quarterly (mixed-frequency) RNNs that read each monthly figure separately as soon as it is out, and compared them with a quarterly-only RNN (monthly figures averaged per quarter), the DFM and an Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA) model, which uses only past GDP." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_networks_en_v2.svg" alt="The same monthly and quarterly figures over three quarters, fed to two RNN set-ups. The quarterly-only RNN averages the three months and combines them with the quarterly figures into one input per quarter: it makes a nowcast every quarter, but not while the current quarter is still incomplete. My monthly + quarterly RNNs read every month as its own input step, followed by the quarterly figures: they make a nowcast every month, even before the quarter ends." full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Data" title="Many indicators, few observations" text="The dataset contains 238 US indicators: 121 monthly ones from FRED-MD and 117 quarterly ones from FRED-QD. GDP is measured quarterly: 260 quarters from 1960 to 2024, of which 160 come before the first test year (year 2000).

With almost as many candidate features as observations, a model is prone to learn random noise in the training data instead of real patterns (overfitting). I therefore used Least Angle Regression (LARS), as recommended by the European Central Bank (ECB) nowcasting toolbox ([Linzenich & Meunier, 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp3004~3ce9d0d8ca.en.pdf)), to keep the 10 to 20 most informative indicators. A few of the top indicators were industrial production, capacity utilisation, employment and consumption. Only a handful of indicators really matter: after the top 11, the coefficients quickly drop towards zero. Leaving most indicators out therefore loses little (right-hand figure).

The data exploration also supported the mixed-frequency idea: monthly figures from the current quarter correlate more strongly with this quarter's GDP growth than those of the previous quarter (left-hand figure: 0.35 one month into the quarter, against 0.19 with only the previous quarter's months)." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_mpl_corr_monthly.png" alt="Three rows of dots, one dot per monthly indicator, showing its absolute correlation with this quarter's GDP growth. With only the previous quarter's months the mean is 0.19; including one month of this quarter it is 0.35, including two months 0.33, and many indicators then reach correlations of 0.6 to 0.8." src2="/assets/img/projects/thesis_mpl_lars_top.png" alt2="Bar chart of the 14 indicators LARS selected on the 1960–1999 training data, ranked by absolute coefficient. Industrial production and capacity utilisation in manufacturing lead, followed by employment in goods-producing industries, manufacturing output and real personal consumption; three of the 14 are quarterly indicators." full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Result question 3" title="Result comparison in expansions vs. recessions" text="**Research question 3:** *How does the models' performance differ between recessions and expansions?*

The results paint a mixed picture. In the 2008 financial crisis the DFM was best, in the milder dot-com recession the quarterly-only RNN, and over COVID-19 the monthly + quarterly RNN just edged out the DFM. In calm periods the monthly + quarterly RNN had the lowest RMSE. Based on this data alone, it is not clear which models perform better in crises. Each recession has a different winner, there are only three recessions of a few quarters each, and looking at the total error over all crisis quarters combined, the monthly + quarterly RNN and the DFM are almost equal (RMSE 2.34 against 2.35)." src="/assets/img/projects/thesis_mpl_crisis_vs_calm.png" alt="Four bar charts of the RMSE per period for the DFM, the quarterly-only RNN and the monthly + quarterly RNN. Dot-com recession: quarterly-only RNN best (0.33). 2008 financial crisis: DFM best (0.68). COVID-19: monthly + quarterly RNN best (4.66) just ahead of the DFM (4.71). Expansions: monthly + quarterly RNN best (0.43), ahead of the quarterly-only RNN (0.48) and the DFM (0.52)." full=true %}

{% include slide.html kicker="Discussion" title="Limitations and future work" points="**Simple comparison model:** my DFM is a simple version, trained on the same data as the deep learning models. Central banks train it on much more and more complex data, so I cannot yet conclude that the deep learning models also outperform the DFM in that setting. | **Little data:** GDP comes out only four times a year and the series start around 1960, while deep learning needs a lot of data. Possible solutions: training on data from several countries, or adding synthetic data, for example simulated with Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models. | **Next step:** a model that makes a nowcast at any moment, using everything known at that time, such as daily financial market data." statement=true %}
